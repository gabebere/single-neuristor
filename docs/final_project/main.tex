\section{Yuanhang-based current-drive validation}

This run checks that the Yuanhang-based implementation can produce a stable current-driven oscillation with a nonzero voltage valley. It validates the simulation mechanism; it is not a fit to the experimental sample.

At $600~\mu$A, the nominal value ($49.6278$~pJ/K) did not sustain oscillations, so it was increased fourfold to slow the thermal response and obtain a stable validation cycle. This control value is not an estimate of our sample's thermal capacitance.

\section{Sample-specific resistance--temperature fit}

The supplied file contains 264 measurements from one cooling sweep and one heating sweep over $289.85$--$370.16$~K at $2~\mu$A. We fitted the major-loop form

\begin{equation}
R(T,\delta)=R_m+R_0 e^{(E_a/k_B)/T}
\left[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\tanh\!\left(\beta\left(\delta\frac{w}{2}+T_c-T\right)\right)\right],
\qquad
\delta=\begin{cases}-1,&\text{cooling},\\+1,&\text{heating}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:sample_rt_fit}
\end{equation}

The fit minimizes the residual in $\log_{10}R$. The minor-loop parameter $\gamma$ was fixed to Yuanhang's value because a major loop alone does not determine it.

Here $R_0$ is the Arrhenius prefactor, not the insulating resistance. On the fully insulating branch,

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{ins}}(T)\approx R_m+R_0e^{(E_a/k_B)/T}.
\end{equation}

For example, the fitted sample gives $R_{\mathrm{ins}}(300~\mathrm{K})\approx3.78~\mathrm{k}\Omega$, well above the metallic floor $R_m=18.22~\Omega$.

\section{Environmental thermal conductance}

The objective is to estimate the environmental thermal conductance $S_e$, which
sets the heat-loss rate $S_e(T-T_0)$. The estimate requires a hot operating point
whose temperature is approximately stationary. We use the following procedure:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Order the measured records by their baseline-corrected input current and
identify the last record without coherent oscillation. This gives the stable point
closest to switching, where the temperature rise is large but a quasi-steady
balance remains defensible.
\item Subtract the pre-pulse current and voltage offsets and select a settled window
in which $V/I$ is nearly constant. In this window $dV/dt\approx0$, so the measured
input current is approximately the resistive current through the VO$_2$ device.
\item Calculate the effective resistance $R_*=V_*/I_*$ and Joule power
$P_*=I_*V_*$. Invert the fitted heating branch, $R_{\mathrm{heat}}(T_*)=R_*$,
to infer the device temperature $T_*$.
\item Apply the quasi-steady thermal balance
$C_{\mathrm{th}}\,dT/dt=P_*-S_e(T_*-T_0)\approx0$, which gives
$S_e=P_*/(T_*-T_0)$.
\end{enumerate}

The filenames identify the nominal source-voltage setting, not the voltage across
the VO$_2$ device and not the simulation input. We therefore retain that setting
only for traceability and use the measured current. Over the common $50$--$250$~ns
analysis window, the 250 and 300~mV source settings produced baseline-corrected
current steps of $189.6$ and $228.2~\mu$A, respectively.

For the selected trace, the current and voltage offsets are their medians from $-200$ to $-50$~ns. We then use the settled $100$--$250$~ns interval. Its effective resistance changes by only $0.145\%$, supporting the quasi-steady approximation. The baseline-corrected medians give

\begin{equation}
I_*=190.162~\mu\mathrm{A},\qquad
V_*=319.213~\mathrm{mV},\qquad
R_*=\frac{V_*}{I_*}=1679.759~\Omega,
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
P_*=I_*V_*=60.658~\mu\mathrm{W}.
\end{equation}

Solving the fitted heating branch $R_{\mathrm{heat}}(T_*)=R_*$ gives $T_*=330.905$~K. At a quasi-steady point, the thermal equation reduces to

\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{th}}\frac{dT}{dt}=P_*-S_e(T_*-T_0)\approx0,
\qquad
S_e=\frac{P_*}{T_*-T_0}.
\end{equation}

Therefore,

\begin{equation}
S_e=\frac{60.658~\mu\mathrm{W}}{330.905~\mathrm{K}-314.4~\mathrm{K}}
=3.675~\mu\mathrm{W/K}
=0.003675~\mathrm{mW/K}.
\label{eq:sample_conductance}
\end{equation}

\section{Electrical capacitance}
\label{sec:electrical_capacitance}

The objective is to estimate the effective electrical capacitance $C$, which stores
charge at the device node and sets the electrical response time
$\tau_{\mathrm{el}}=R(T)C$. In the ideal-current model, the measured input current
splits between the VO$_2$ resistance and this capacitance:

\begin{equation}
I_{\mathrm{in}}=\frac{V}{R(T)}+C\frac{dV}{dt}.
\end{equation}

We therefore seek the part of the measured current that cannot be explained by
the instantaneous resistive current $V/R(T)$. The analysis uses low-current,
non-switching rising edges so that the device begins on the known cold heating
branch and a phase transition does not complicate the electrical transient. It
assumes that the current and voltage channels are time-aligned, their pre-pulse
offsets are removable, $C$ is approximately constant over the fit window, and the
reduced circuit contains the relevant current paths. The procedure is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Subtract each trace's pre-pulse current and voltage medians and select the
initial rising edge of the 100--250~mV source-setting records, all of which remain
below switching.
\item Use $C_{\mathrm{shortcut}}=\Delta I/(dV/dt)$ as a preliminary scale estimate.
This expression is valid only if the resistive current is negligible; it must not
be accepted before checking the full current balance.
\item Evaluate the physically complete residual
$I_C=I_{\mathrm{in}}-V/R(T)$ using the fitted cold resistance, and fit
$I_C=C\,dV/dt$ over the early edge with the physical constraint $C\geq0$.
\item Check the result independently against the measured current--voltage timing.
If no positive lag is resolved, convert the timing resolution $\Delta t$ into an
upper bound $C\lesssim\Delta t/R(T_0)$. We adopt that bound as a conservative
modeling value while retaining $C=0$ as the best-fit lower endpoint.
\end{enumerate}

We first reproduce the preliminary shortcut. For each trace, $\Delta I$ is the
baseline-to-plateau current step and $dV/dt$ is a linear fit over 0--30~ns, giving

\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{shortcut}}=\frac{\Delta I}{dV/dt},
\qquad
\frac{\mu\mathrm{A}}{\mathrm{mV/ns}}=\mathrm{pF}.
\end{equation}

The median shortcut estimate is $19.8$~pF, but it is biased because the measured current itself rises over $26$--$27$~ns and most of it flows resistively. Applying the full current balance gives

\begin{equation}
C\frac{dV}{dt}=I_{\mathrm{in}}-\frac{V}{R(T)},
\qquad
C=\frac{I_{\mathrm{in}}-V/R(T)}{dV/dt}.
\end{equation}

At $T_0=314.4$~K, the fitted heating branch gives $R(T_0)=2.570$~k$\Omega$. Substituting the measured current and voltage leaves no resolved capacitive current: the unconstrained early-edge fit is approximately $-0.25$ to $-0.10$~pF, depending on the fit window, and the physical nonnegative optimum is therefore $C=0$. The current and voltage 50\% crossings differ by only $-0.74$ to $+0.52$~ns. Taking 1~ns as the timing resolution gives the non-statistical bound

\begin{equation}
C\lesssim\frac{1~\mathrm{ns}}{2.570~\mathrm{k}\Omega}\approx0.39~\mathrm{pF}.
\end{equation}

In contrast, $19.8$~pF would give $R(T_0)C=50.9$~ns and a large voltage lag that is absent from the data. A larger capacitance would worsen the disagreement. This does not establish that the physical capacitance is exactly zero; it means that a positive value is not identifiable from the present traces. To avoid assuming an instantaneous electrical response, the sample-specific simulations and downstream thermal fit use the conservative upper-bound value

\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{adopt}}=0.39~\mathrm{pF},
\qquad
I_R=I_{\mathrm{in}}-C_{\mathrm{adopt}}\frac{dV}{dt}.
\end{equation}

This is not a measured central estimate: $C=0$ remains the constrained best fit,
and $0.39$~pF intentionally maximizes the electrical memory still compatible with
the approximately 1~ns timing resolution.

This conclusion is independent of the present choice of $\gamma$. The capacitance fit uses the initial cold heating edge, before a temperature reversal; $\gamma$ modifies only minor hysteresis loops after a reversal and therefore does not enter this calculation. Changing $\gamma$ can alter later oscillation shapes and frequencies, but it cannot supply the missing early-edge voltage lag. A numerical nonzero estimate requires a faster, low-amplitude nonswitching edge with calibrated current--voltage channel timing; with $R$ approximately constant, $C=\tau_{\mathrm{el}}/R$.

\section{Thermal time constant and thermal capacitance}
\label{sec:thermal_capacitance}

The objective is to estimate the effective thermal capacitance $C_{\mathrm{th}}$,
which relates net heating power to the device's temperature rate of change. Together
with the environmental conductance found in Section~4, it sets the thermal response
time

\begin{equation}
\tau_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{th}}}{S_e}.
\end{equation}

A larger $C_{\mathrm{th}}$ means that more energy is required to change the device
temperature and therefore produces a slower thermal response. Section~5 found no
resolved positive electrical capacitance but established the upper bound
$C_{\mathrm{adopt}}=0.39$~pF. We use this conservative value and first separate the
resistive current from the capacitive current. The fitted heating branch then acts
as a thermometer:

\begin{equation}
I_R(t)=I_*(t)-C_{\mathrm{adopt}}\frac{dV_*}{dt},
\qquad
R_*(t)=\frac{V_*(t)}{I_R(t)},
\qquad
T_*(t)=R_{\mathrm{heat}}^{-1}\!\left(R_*(t)\right).
\end{equation}

The estimate assumes a single lumped device temperature, constant $C_{\mathrm{th}}$
and $S_e$ over the fitted interval, the same sample for the electrical and $R(T)$
measurements, and validity of the fitted heating branch before any thermal reversal.
It is conditional on the adopted $0.39$~pF upper bound and requires
baseline-corrected, time-aligned current and voltage channels. The procedure is:

\begin{enumerate}
\item Select moderate non-switching traces with sufficient signal, subtract their
pre-pulse offsets, and smooth the oscilloscope noise before evaluating $dV_*/dt$
and forming $V_*/I_R$. We
use measured current steps of $76.2$, $114.2$, and $152.2~\mu$A (the 100, 150,
and 200~mV source settings); the $189.6~\mu$A record (250~mV setting) is reserved
as a near-transition sensitivity check.
\item Compute $I_R=I_*-C_{\mathrm{adopt}}dV_*/dt$, reconstruct the measured
temperature trajectory from $R_*(t)=V_*(t)/I_R(t)$ and the inverse fitted heating
branch, and calculate the applied Joule power $P_*(t)=I_R(t)V_*(t)$.
\item For trial values of $C_{\mathrm{th}}$, integrate the thermal balance using
the previously estimated $S_e$ and $T_0$. Fit one shared $C_{\mathrm{th}}$ by
minimizing the temperature mismatch across all three primary traces over
15--35~ns, then obtain $\tau_{\mathrm{th}}=C_{\mathrm{th}}/S_e$.
\item Test robustness by fitting each trace separately, checking the excluded
$189.6~\mu$A record, varying the fit window, and propagating the $R(T)$, $S_e$,
ambient temperature, and waveform uncertainties.
\end{enumerate}

The thermal trajectory for each trial value is obtained from

\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{th}}\frac{dT}{dt}=P_*(t)-S_e(T-T_0),
\qquad
C_{\mathrm{th}}=S_e\tau_{\mathrm{th}}.
\end{equation}

A nine-sample, second-order Savitzky--Golay filter is used in the reconstruction.
Because the fit ends before a thermal reversal, $\gamma$ does not enter this
estimate. Figure~\ref{fig:thermal_capacitance} compares the temperatures inferred
directly from the electrical measurements with the trajectories predicted by the
shared thermal model.

Relative to the $C=0$ analysis, the upper-bound correction raises the central $C_{\mathrm{th}}$ estimate by only $1.5\%$ ($0.047163$ to $0.047873$~pJ/K), although the RMSE increases from $0.806$ to $1.145$~K. The $189.6~\mu$A trace gives a smaller value and a substantially larger error because it approaches a reversal; including it would bias the single-heating-branch fit downward. The fitted $13.0$~ns thermal time is of the same order as the measured 16--24~ns oscillation period. Yuanhang's thermal capacitance is approximately $1037$ times larger.

The experimental manuscript reports a 150~nm film thickness and an approximately 200~nm electrode gap \cite{gildor2026}, but not the electrically active filament width or volume. A numerical geometry estimate $C_{\mathrm{th}}=\rho c_pV$ would therefore introduce an unconstrained volume and is not reported.

\section{Capacitance dependence and the thermal-only limit}
\label{sec:capacitance_dependence}

In the zero-capacitance limit,

\begin{equation}
C=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V(t)=I_{\mathrm{in}}(t)R(T),
\qquad
\tau_{\mathrm{th}}=\frac{C_{\mathrm{th}}}{S_e}.
\end{equation}

A thermal relaxation cycle requires the same current to heat through the heating
transition and then cool through the cooling transition. Using the fitted branch
resistances at the two transition centers gives

\begin{equation}
I>I_{\mathrm{heat}}
=\sqrt{\frac{S_e(T_{\mathrm{heat}}-T_0)}{R_{\mathrm{heat}}}}
=329.65~\mu\mathrm{A},
\qquad
I<I_{\mathrm{cool}}
=\sqrt{\frac{S_e(T_{\mathrm{cool}}-T_0)}{R_{\mathrm{cool}}}}
=254.44~\mu\mathrm{A}.
\end{equation}

Because $I_{\mathrm{heat}}>I_{\mathrm{cool}}$, these conditions do not overlap:
the fitted system has no thermal-only oscillation window. At the adopted
$C=0.39$~pF, the metallic electrical time is $CR_m=7.10$~ps, compared with
$\tau_{\mathrm{th}}=13.026$~ns. The ratio is $5.45\times10^{-4}$, so this
capacitance supplies negligible electrical memory and does not alter the steady
thermal thresholds.

The numerical study varies $C$ from $0$ to $50$~pF and $C_{\mathrm{th}}$ across
its conditional $0.021918$--$0.092624$~pJ/K interval while holding every other
parameter fixed. The experimentally admissible range $0\le C\le0.39$~pF produces
no oscillation anywhere in that interval. Oscillations first appear at $3$~pF only
at the lowest tested $C_{\mathrm{th}}$; at the adopted $C_{\mathrm{th}}$, they first
appear at $7$~pF. Both values exceed the electrical timing bound and would create a
voltage lag absent from the measured edge.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../../public_jobs/20260829_100718_specimen-model-prediction-versus-measured-curren_eefab7/figures/capacitance_sensitivity.png}
\caption{Predicted frequency under capacitance variation. Gray denotes no coherent
oscillation. The dashed line and red cross identify the adopted electrical and thermal
capacitances; larger electrical values are stress tests outside the timing bound.}
\label{fig:capacitance_sensitivity}
\end{figure}

The present parameter set therefore matches the stable pre-onset voltage but cannot
reproduce the measured oscillatory regime without an electrical capacitance that
contradicts the edge timing. The next useful tests are an independently measured
dynamic switching loop and inclusion of the actual TIA/load impedance. Changing
$\gamma$ alone cannot explain the onset at $228.2~\mu$A, because the predicted
temperature there does not reach the first heating transition; $\gamma$ acts only
after a hysteresis reversal.

\section{Global parameter inference from all measured waveforms}
\label{sec:global_inference}

We next asked whether one shared set of model parameters could be inferred directly
from the complete current--voltage data set. This is a stricter test than fitting one
trace: the same vector

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(C,C_{\mathrm{th}},S_e,T_0,T_c,w,\beta,\gamma)
\end{equation}

must describe all 22 measured current waveforms. The source settings at 200, 400,
600, 800, and 1000~mV were withheld from optimization, leaving 17 training traces
and five blind test traces. The source-setting labels only identify records; the
measured current waveform is the model input in every case.

For each candidate, the simulator uses the complete measured current transient and
compares the predicted and measured voltage over the common 50--250~ns plateau. The
weighted objective is

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
J={}&0.20J_{\mathrm{wave}}+0.20J_{\mathrm{mean}}+0.15J_{\mathrm{amp}}
+0.15J_{\mathrm{spec}}\\
&+0.15J_f+0.10J_{\mathrm{class}}+0.05J_{\mathrm{edge}}+0.05J_{\mathrm{prior}}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:global_objective}
\end{equation}

Here $J_{\mathrm{wave}}$ is a normalized waveform error, with a $\pm12$~ns phase
shift allowed only for oscillatory records; the other terms compare mean voltage,
peak-to-peak amplitude, normalized spectrum, frequency, oscillation classification,
and the 0--50~ns rising edge. The constrained fit includes a weak prior and keeps
each parameter inside its independently estimated interval. A second, deliberately
relaxed fit removes that prior and expands the bounds to test whether the model can
fit the data only by abandoning those measurements. Differential evolution explores
the global space, followed by bounded local refinement. Optimization uses
$\Delta t=0.2$~ns; every reported candidate is rerun at $0.05$~ns.

\section{Oscillation-priority parameter inference}
\label{sec:oscillation_priority}

The balanced objective in Section~\ref{sec:global_inference} rewards average waveform
agreement, so the optimizer can prefer a turn-on transient over sustained oscillation.
We therefore repeated the shared-parameter fit with oscillation tracking as the first
priority. A sinusoid at each measured oscillation frequency is fitted separately in
four consecutive plateau segments. A candidate receives persistence credit only when
that periodic component remains present throughout the plateau.

The final objective is

\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
J={}&250J_{\mathrm{class}}+250J_{\mathrm{false+}}+0.50J_f
+0.50J_{\mathrm{sustain}}\\
&+0.05J_{\mathrm{spec}}+0.02J_{\mathrm{wave}}+0.02J_{\mathrm{mean}}
+0.01J_{\mathrm{amp}}+0.01J_{\mathrm{edge}}+0.01J_{\mathrm{prior}}.
\end{split}
\label{eq:oscillation_priority_objective}
\end{equation}

Thus one classification error dominates all ordinary waveform terms. A false
oscillation outside the measured window contributes to both $J_{\mathrm{class}}$ and
$J_{\mathrm{false+}}$, so it costs twice as much as missing one boundary record. The
search was performed directly at $\Delta t=0.05$~ns. The development sequence was:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A $0.2$~ns scouting fit produced nine apparent oscillatory records, but only
three survived at $0.05$~ns; this candidate was rejected.
\item A direct $0.05$~ns fit produced five consecutive oscillatory records and
classified 16 of 22 records correctly.
\item A full-resolution $C$--$C_{\mathrm{th}}$ neighborhood identified a training-set
seed at $C=8$~pF and $C_{\mathrm{th}}=0.020$~pJ/K.
\item Differential evolution and bounded local refinement optimized the remaining
secondary errors while preserving the best classification basin.
\end{enumerate}

The final optimization still excludes the five source settings used previously for
testing. Because those records had already been inspected during earlier model
development, their results are useful withheld-current checks but are not claimed as
a pristine blind test.

\section{Oscillation-amplitude tuning}
\label{sec:oscillation_amplitude}

The classification-first fit recovers the oscillation window, but its voltage cycles
are too large. We therefore repeated the same eight-parameter search with two explicit
amplitude terms. The absolute term compares peak-to-peak voltages directly, while

\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{relamp}}=
\left[\ln\!\left(\frac{V_{\mathrm{pp,pred}}+3~\mathrm{mV}}
{V_{\mathrm{pp,meas}}+3~\mathrm{mV}}\right)\right]^2
\end{equation}

penalizes their ratio, so small and large measured cycles matter comparably. The
$3$~mV floor only keeps the logarithm finite near zero. This term is evaluated only
for experimentally oscillatory records. The objective used weights of 2500 for
classification and false positives, 10 for relative amplitude, 1 for absolute
amplitude, 0.5 for frequency, 0.1 each for waveform, mean voltage, and spectrum, 1
for persistence, and 0.02 for the turn-on edge.

$0.025$ and $0.0125$~ns. Table~\ref{tab:amplitude_tradeoff} compares both diagnostic
